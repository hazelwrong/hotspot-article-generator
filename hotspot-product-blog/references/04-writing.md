# Prompt 04: Structure and prose

Write a complete English article around the consumer task, not a review or fixed seven-section template every time. The title, opening answer, sections, and conclusion must serve the same achievable promise.

Help readers locate their choice or method immediately. Keep trend background only as long as needed to explain the demand; omit extended celebrity biography, sports history, and news unrelated to product decisions. Do not force “replacing,” “best,” “tested,” or a year into the title to manufacture a promise.

Choose structure by intent: comparisons need aligned criteria and scenario conclusions; tutorials need conditions, tools, actions, and checks; why articles need explanations and buying/use implications; inspiration needs visual approaches and transferable principles. A desk-researched review must clearly state its evidence basis. Do not add unnecessary sections, tables, or products merely to match the sample.

Put information gain in the reader-facing body, not only in review notes. Provide usable answers to all key questions. Do not end an explainable judgment with “do your own research” or “it depends on your needs.” State precise data limits without repeating disclaimers excessively.

Explain using actual products, not category generalities alone. Compare candidates on aligned fields and leave unknowns unknown. Never infer every product's properties from a category label: a 24-inch cropped barn jacket is a counterexample to “barn jackets are always longer than aviators.”

## Section roles and necessary repetition

Before drafting, choose an article architecture from the reader's task and evidence, and briefly record why it fits. Identify where a product resolves a question or enables a step. Do not default to criteria/comparison → two adjacent product cards → checklist → conclusion. Site names do not prescribe article templates, and changing headings alone does not change structure.

Possible approaches, not required templates: a tutorial introduces materials where steps need them; troubleshooting follows diagnostic branches and offers replacements only when warranted; inspiration develops usable arrangements and places products within them; a genuine comparison aligns alternatives on relevant criteria. Select product number and placement from coverage needs, not a quota. A single suitable item or several complementary items can be more appropriate than two competing products.

In batches, inspect whether outlines and product placements are repeated for a substantive reason. Keep shared visual styles if useful; vary organization only when the consumer task warrants it, not for cosmetic novelty. Neither structure variation nor new formatting counts as information gain.

Use same-basis tables for comparisons, numbered steps for sequences, concise lists for parallel conditions/costs, and prose for causation, evidence limits, and complex tradeoffs. Do not impose table or FAQ counts. During revision, preserve useful unique information under the retention checks in 02-research.md rather than summarizing it away.

Tables enable quick comparison; cards explain a particular product's rationale and tradeoffs; styling or procedural sections show application; checklists explain verification; conclusions resolve the decision. A fact may recur briefly for navigation or safety, but each extended treatment should serve a distinct purpose.

Remove duplicated explanations, generic conclusions, template transitions, and summaries with no new function. Do not cut evidence, essential conditions, or added value merely to shorten the piece. Set no fixed reduction percentage or word count. Retain repetition needed for independently readable cards, safe execution, or returning to a decision.

Use clear, restrained sentences. Distinguish facts, inference, and insufficient evidence; “unverified” must not become “absent.” Without real testing, never write “we found in testing” or “our reviewer wore.” Length follows the work needed to solve the reader's problem.
